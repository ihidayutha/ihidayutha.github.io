Though scholars are now agreed that sufi is derived from suf (wool), the similarity in sound of suffa encouraged the legend, and it is said that a sufi is one who resembles the ahl al-suffa.

Sufism (at-tasawwuf) is therefore the search for divine wisdom through purification. It is an initiation into a path in which reality has an external aspect (ad'dhahir) and an internal aspect (al'bātine). As a result, for some, this path is not always easy to understand because it is open to a certain universalism and rejects any dogmatic reduction.

Legalism, in its extreme forms, eventually led to legislation "in God’s name" across every sphere of public and private life. The meticulous observance of commandments demanded by certain jurists compelled the faithful to submit completely to the Law, as thus understood and constructed, rather than to God Himself. 

As the Law became an object of idolatry, certain Sufis throughout the centuries opposed this strict legalism with varying degrees of success. 

The great mystic al-Hallaj paid with his life for asserting that one who attains divine proximity is no longer bound by ritual practices. 

Conversely, Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani was more prudent; he tempered his statements by drawing on various arguments and adopting subtle accommodations to better convey his message. 

The Sufi writings of Emir Abdelkader remained virtually obscured until recently. As for Ibn ‘Arabi, from the 12th century to the present day, he remains a stumbling block, a dividing line, a figure regarding whom almost every person conversant with these matters must take a stand. 

Ibn al-'Arabi is known as the Great Sheik among the surviving Sufi orders. Born in Muslim Spain, he has become famous in the West as the greatest mystical thinker of Islamic civilization. He was a great philosopher, theologian, and poet. 

William Chittick takes a major step toward exposing the breadth and depth of Ibn al-'Arabi's vision. The book offers his view of spiritual perfection and explains his theology, ontology, epistemology, hermeneutics, and soteriology. The clear language, unencumbered by methodological jargon, makes it accessible to those familiar with other spiritual traditions, while its scholarly precision will appeal to specialists. 

Beginning with a survey of Ibn al-'Arabi's major teachings, the book gradually introduces the most important facets of his thought, devoting attention to definitions of his basic terminology. His teachings are illustrated with many translated passages introducing readers to fascinating byways of spiritual life that would not ordinarily be encountered in an account of a thinker's ideas. Ibn al-'Arabi is allowed to describe in detail the visionary world from which his knowledge derives and to express his teachings in his own words. 

More than 600 passages from his major work, al-Futuhat al-Makkivva, are translated here, practically for the first time. These alone provide twice the text of the Fusus al-hikam. The exhaustive indexes make the work an invaluable reference tool for research in Sufism and Islamic thought in general.
